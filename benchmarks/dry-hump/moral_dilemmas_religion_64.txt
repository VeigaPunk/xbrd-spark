If “good” is defined solely as whatever God commands, can a command to inflict pointless cruelty still be called good, or does that collapse morality into raw power?
When a later revelation recants an earlier command that believers already obeyed at great cost, were those first acts ever righteous, or only provisionally binding until superseded?
If a private locution orders a believer to lie to civil authorities to protect the congregation, does the source of the command override the prohibition on false witness, or must the command be tested against the tradition’s own moral core?
When two equally sincere prophets issue contradictory divine commands, what could justify treating one as authentic without already assuming an independent standard of discernment?
If the only evidence that a command is divine is the believer’s inner certainty, does that certainty have moral authority over the foreseeable harm the command would cause?
Should a community treat a command as non-binding if obeying it would serve no good except the demonstration of obedience itself?
If God could have achieved the same spiritual end without requiring an innocent’s death, does using that death as a test make the command morally defective even if the speaker is God?
When civil law requires reporting ongoing child abuse but a pastor learns of it in a confidential pastoral conversation that is not a sacramental confession, does professional secrecy still bind, or does the child’s safety dissolve the privilege?
If a theocratic court sentences an apostate to death under revealed law that the majority sincerely accepts, is the sentence an exercise of communal self-rule or a rights violation no popular piety can authorize?
Should a secular state exempt a ritual blade, sacred intoxicant, or gender-segregated space from generally applicable safety and equality laws, or does equal citizenship require the same rules for sacred and profane practice?
When a religious arbitration agreement signed under family pressure waives a spouse’s access to civil divorce courts, is enforcing that waiver respect for religious liberty or coercion dressed as consent?
If criminalizing blasphemy would reduce pogroms against a vulnerable minority, does preventing violence justify privileging sacred speech, or does the criminalization itself mark believers as too fragile for equal civic standing?
Should a believer refuse military service that would compel them to kill, even if the war meets just-war criteria their co-religionists accept, or does public defense override private conscientious objection?
If a faith-based hospital is the only emergency facility in a region and doctrine forbids a life-saving procedure, does institutional religious liberty include the right to let the patient die, or does monopoly care create a duty to treat?
When a parent cites religious liberty to withhold a proven vaccine from a child during an outbreak, whose claim wins: parental piety, the child’s bodily future, or the neighbors who will share the risk?
If historical-critical scholarship shows that a founding miracle was a later pious invention, does intellectual honesty require public recantation, private demythologizing, or continued profession for the sake of the weak in faith?
Should a believer keep teaching children a literal cosmology they themselves have abandoned, because the story still binds the community, or is that a form of pious fraud?
If two religions produce equally compassionate lives while making incompatible exclusive truth claims, what, if anything, could justify choosing one as true rather than as a moral practice?
When archaeological evidence under a shrine would falsify a central origin story, is excavation a duty to truth or a desecration that the community may rightly forbid?
If faith is counted as righteous precisely because it is held against the balance of evidence, is that virtue or a trained refusal of the only tools that correct error?
Should a cleric who has lost belief continue to officiate, because the rites still comfort the dying, or does leading worship without belief profane both the office and the congregation?
If natural theology and mystical experience point toward incompatible pictures of God, which has standing to correct the other, and who is authorized to decide?
When a sacred text’s plain sense endorses slavery, conquest, or the legal inferiority of women, is the moral move to obey, to reinterpret until the text says the opposite, or to demote the text’s authority?
If an omnipotent benevolent God could have created a world with free will and far less horrific suffering, does actual suffering count as evidence against that God, or only as a mystery the believer must not weigh?
Is continuing to worship after watching preventable mass death a virtue of fidelity, a failure of moral perception, or a psychologically necessary fiction that should not be called knowledge?
If suffering is said to be required for spiritual growth, does preventing a child’s pain become an act of spiritual theft, or is the growth-story a post-hoc excuse for preventable harm?
When a parent chooses prayer and refuses evidence-based treatment for a child’s treatable illness, is that martyrdom by proxy, legitimate religious medical pluralism, or assault?
If petitionary prayer changes nothing except the petitioner’s affect, does the practice retain religious worth, or does its value then depend on a false theory of how the world works?
Does persistent divine silence in the face of sincere, desperate prayer count against the claim that God is personal and responsive, or is silence itself a form of pedagogy the sufferer is not entitled to reject?
If a preacher interprets a natural disaster as punishment for a stigmatized group, is repeating that claim prophetic truth-telling or a libel that weaponizes grief?
Should a believer accept that an infant’s death was “God’s will,” or does attributing the death to God make the believer complicit in a story that excuses the universe at the infant’s expense?
When leaving a faith would devastate aging parents who defined their lives by transmitting it, does filial piety require remaining, or does using the child’s conscience as a family monument violate the child?
If a community requires shunning a member who married outside the faith, is compliance loyalty to a revealed boundary or participation in a punishment no adult’s marriage can deserve?
Is converting in order to obtain safety, a visa, or a marriage license a legitimate use of religious form, a coerced void act, or a betrayal that the convert’s former community may justly refuse to forgive?
When disaster aid is bundled with baptism, does accepting the rite under hunger count as valid conversion, survival without religious meaning, or a theft of the recipient’s spiritual agency?
Do parents have the right to inscribe an irreversible religious mark on an infant who cannot consent, or does the child’s later liberty require delaying belonging until it can be chosen?
If the only way out of a high-control religious group is to be publicly denounced as apostate, with family remaining inside, is writing a tell-all a duty to warn others or a sacrifice of the people still captive?
Can a person inhabit two exclusive traditions at once as an act of peace, or does syncretism evade the truth-claim that makes each tradition more than a lifestyle?
When a dying spouse asks you to raise the children in a faith you now reject, does the deathbed promise bind, or would keeping it make you an instrument of a creed you consider false?
If a holy war is the only remaining way to prevent the extermination of a people, does divine authorization add moral weight, or is the justice of the war fully settled by necessity, discrimination, and proportionality without God?
When a pacifist’s refusal to fight would allow a massacre they could stop, is remaining nonviolent fidelity to a revealed counsel of perfection, or a choice to prefer clean hands over the lives of the unprotected?
Does choosing martyrdom remain courageous if the death leaves dependents without protection, or does the spectacle of witness then consume people who never volunteered?
If a mob will kill outsiders unless a sacred image is destroyed, should the community surrender the image, or does yielding teach that violence is an effective liturgy?
When a tradition treats certain animals as the proper victims of sacrifice, does animal welfare have standing against revealed rite, or is that standing a secular imperialism over the sacred?
If defending a shrine with lethal force is the only way to prevent its demolition, is the land’s holiness a just cause, or does killing for a place confuse the map of the holy with the map of blood?
Should a believer treat honor-based violence framed as religious duty as a misreading that must be denounced from inside the tradition, or as authentic piety that outsiders have no standing to judge?
If a prophecy can be fulfilled only through political assassination, must the prophecy be rejected as ungodly, or does successful fulfillment retroactively vindicate the means?
