Can a war remain just when civilian deaths are foreseeable but unintended, and what scale of collateral harm voids any claim of discrimination?

Carefully distinguishes intention, proportionality, necessity, and civilian-risk thresholds in just-war reasoning.picker-1

I'll check the workspace for any task context, then answer the just-war question directly.This is a just-war ethics question, not a coding task. I’ll answer it on the doctrine, then on where scale actually breaks discrimination.Yes — a war can remain just when civilian deaths are foreseeable but not intended. Discrimination is about what you aim at, not about whether anyone dies. Scale does not by itself convert a side-effect into a target. What voids discrimination is not a body count. It is when civilian harm becomes the object, the means, or a pretext so dominant that “we were aiming at a military objective” is no longer a true description of the act.

Those are two different failures, and mixing them is how the doctrine gets laundered.

Discrimination is not a feeling of regret

Just war theory (jus in bello) and the law of armed conflict split the question:

ConstraintWhat it asksWhat it does not ask
Discrimination (distinction)Is the object of attack a military objective? Are civilians and civilian objects not the target?Did you expect civilians to die? Did you feel bad?
ProportionalityIs the expected incidental civilian harm excessive relative to the concrete, direct military advantage?Was the war just overall? Were the civilians “innocent enough”?
PrecautionsDid you take all feasible steps to verify the target and minimize civilian harm?Did the other side hide among civilians?

Foreseeable-but-unintended civilian death is the doctrine of double effect (Aquinas → Vitoria/Suárez → Walzer). An act with two effects — one sought (destroying a gun battery), one foreseen and unwanted (killing people in the next building) — can be permissible if:

  1. the act itself is not already evil (you are attacking a genuine military objective);
  2. you do not intend the bad effect, either as end or as means;
  3. the good is not produced by means of the bad (the civilians’ deaths are not what makes the attack work);
  4. there is a proportionately grave reason.

Condition 2 is the easy one to fake. “We did not want them dead” is almost always true of public statements. Walzer’s correction is the important one: the second intention has to be real. You must also intend to minimize the harm — choose a different weapon, a different hour, a different axis, abort when the pattern of life is denser than the estimate, accept more risk to your own forces. A commander who says “unintended” while picking the cheapest, widest, most deniable method has not satisfied double effect. He has satisfied a press officer.

International humanitarian law tracks this almost exactly. Additional Protocol I, Articles 48, 51, and 57: civilians shall not be the object of attack; indiscriminate attacks are prohibited; constant care and feasible precautions are required; an attack is still unlawful if expected civilian harm is “excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated.” The Rome Statute’s war-crime formulation is “clearly excessive.” Neither text gives a ratio.

Scale voids proportionality. It voids discrimination only when the description of the act collapses

There is no number — not 1:1, not 10:1, not “X civilians per high-value target” — that the tradition or the law treats as a switch.

That is not a loophole. It is because the relevant comparison is not “civilians versus combatants killed.” It is expected civilian harm versus the concrete military advantage of this attack, judged ex ante from information reasonably available. Destroying a radar that is about to vector an airstrike on a city is not the same act as dropping the same munition on a suspected squad in a packed market. Same civilian count can be lawful in one case and criminal in the other.

Where scale does void the claim of discrimination:

1. The “military objective” is a pretext. If the expected civilian harm so dwarfs the military gain that no honest description of the choice is “we attacked the battery,” discrimination has already failed. You used a lawful category as cover. Area bombing of cities to break civilian morale, “dehousing,” nuclear city-busting, and some siege tactics that treat starvation of a population as the method of reducing a garrison, fail here even when the communiqué names a factory.

2. Civilian death is the means, not the side-effect. Double effect dies the moment the good is produced by the civilians dying — terror, coerced surrender, punishment, “they’ll think twice.” That is not collateral. That is using people. Nagel’s point in “War and Massacre” still holds: you may not treat persons as material.

3. The method cannot discriminate, so the intention is idle. ** Saturation bombing of a mixed urban grid, unguided munitions into a dense neighborhood when precision alternatives exist, firing into a crowd because a fighter is somewhere in it: the weapon or pattern cannot distinguish. IHL calls these indiscriminate attacks. Your mental reservation does not reclassify them.

4. Recklessness collapses “unintended” into the moral equivalent of intent. If you know the estimate, reject feasible precautions, and proceed because civilian death is acceptable as a cost of doing business, the law’s word is still “incidental,” but the ethics is willful blindness. Dolus eventualis / practical certainty is not a side-effect. Walzer again: you owe civilians a “second intention” of care, including accepting more risk to soldiers. A force that systematically transfers all risk onto civilians has stopped discriminating even if every target packet has a grid reference.

5. Human shields do not transfer the crime, but they do not license a massacre either. The defender who uses civilians as cover violates discrimination. The attacker does not thereby acquire a blank check. The expected incidental harm is still measured against the advantage of this strike. “They started it” is not a threshold.

What the threshold actually is

State it without a fake number:

  • Discrimination is void when civilians are the object of the attack, when the method cannot distinguish, or when the military-objective story is a cover for producing civilian harm.
  • Proportionality is void when the expected incidental harm is excessive relative to the concrete and direct advantage of that attack — not the justice of the war, not “victory,” not “deterrence in general.”
  • The war’s jus in bello standing is lost for those acts, and can be lost for the campaign if the pattern is systematic. A just cause does not wash indiscriminate or disproportionate conduct. That is the whole point of splitting ad bellum from in bello.
  • There is no numeric kill-ratio that, by itself, proves discrimination failed. A low civilian count can still be murder (a targeted café bombing). A high count can still be a lawful side-effect (a strike on an active air-defense node in a city you tried to evacuate, with the smallest munition that would work, at the hour of lowest density). The test is the structure of the act, not the obituary total.

The honest uncomfortable remainder: “excessive” is judged, not calculated. Tribunals, after-action reviews, and historians will disagree at the margin. That disagreement is not a license to treat the margin as the whole page. The easy cases are not hard — flattening a residential block to kill one gunman, announcing “unintended,” and calling it discrimination. That claim is already void, whatever the communiqué says.

If you want the rule in one line: unintended does not mean unlimited; foreseeable does not mean intended; and a collateral body count voids discrimination only when it reveals that civilians were, in practice, the point.

Should clinical trials of addiction drugs include pregnant people, or is exclusion a greater injustice because dosing remains guesswork?

Reframes exclusion from pregnancy trials as uncontrolled real-world experimentation and offers concrete ethical safeguards.picker-1

This is a live ethics-and-evidence question, so I’m checking current trial policy and what is actually known about addiction-drug dosing in pregnancy before answering.Exclusion as the default is the greater injustice. For addiction medications, categorical exclusion does not protect pregnant people or fetuses. It moves the experiment out of a monitored trial and into everyday care, where dosing is already guesswork and the alternative is often untreated addiction.

That is not an argument for putting every first-in-human compound into a pregnant person. It is an argument against treating pregnancy as a reason to never generate the data that clinicians are already using.

The protection story is backwards

The usual rationale is post-thalidomide caution: do not expose a fetus to an unproven drug. That impulse is understandable. The problem is what happens next.

Pregnant people already take medicines. About 70% take at least one prescription drug in pregnancy. For opioid use disorder, methadone and buprenorphine are first-line care from ACOG, SAMHSA, and CDC, because untreated opioid use in pregnancy is itself a high-risk exposure: overdose, withdrawal, infection, preterm birth, and chaotic prenatal care. Naltrexone is used with much thinner evidence; ACOG’s position is essentially shared decision-making under uncertainty.

So the choice is not “trial versus no exposure.” It is “trial versus unregulated exposure.” Alyssa Bilinski and colleagues put it cleanly: every time a pregnant person takes an untested medication, that is already an uncontrolled experiment. In a trial, someone is watching and someone learns. WHO estimates that only about 4% of trials over the past decade even allowed pregnant participants.

The 2024 National Academies report is even blunter on the liability excuse. Their case-law review found no claims since 1962 for injuries from pregnant or lactating people in trials. They found many claims from people who used already-marketed drugs without trial data. Fear of being sued for studying the question has been used to justify never answering it.

Addiction makes the knowledge gap especially costly

Pregnancy changes pharmacokinetics hard enough that adult labels are not a safe translation:

  • Volume of distribution rises.
  • Hepatic enzymes (including CYP3A4 and CYP2B6) are induced.
  • Methadone clearance often increases and half-life shortens, especially in the third trimester.
  • The practical result is afternoon or evening withdrawal on once-daily clinic dosing, fetal stress, and a higher chance of return to illicit opioids.

Split dosing is widely recommended for that reason. The supporting evidence is still sparse observational work, not adequate PK/PD trials. Underdosing is not a conservative fetal-protection strategy. It is a relapse strategy. Trying to keep the dose “as low as possible” to spare the neonate also fails: NAS severity does not reliably track maternal methadone dose, so dose-minimization does not buy neonatal safety.

Buprenorphine is better studied than naltrexone, but even there the evidence is lumpy. The MOTHER trial compared two active treatments already in use. A 2022 NEJM analysis found buprenorphine associated with better neonatal outcomes than methadone, with similar maternal outcomes — the kind of comparative result you only get if you study the people who actually need the drug. Extended-release formulations, naltrexone, alcohol pharmacotherapies, and almost everything for stimulant use disorder remain closer to anecdote plus postpartum extrapolation.

That is the “dosing remains guesswork” problem in concrete form. The clinician is not choosing caution. They are choosing a dose from a non-pregnant label, then adjusting by withdrawal scores, patient report, and whatever the clinic’s rules allow.

The ethical mistake is treating pregnant people as unable to consent to knowledge

Anne Drapkin Lyerly, Maggie Little, and Ruth Faden’s “second wave” argument is still the right frame: after women were finally included in research as non-pregnant adults, pregnancy was left behind as a special forbidden zone. The 2018 Common Rule even removed pregnant people from the list of “vulnerable populations,” yet Subpart B and institutional habit still function as a presumption of exclusion.

That presumption fails several justice tests at once:

  • It denies people who will be treated anyway a chance to be treated under observation. For methadone and buprenorphine, the ethical trial is usually not placebo. It is PK, dose-timing, formulation, and comparative effectiveness.
  • It concentrates risk on people already least able to absorb it. Pregnant people with addiction already face criminalization, child-welfare surveillance, and stigma. They then receive care that is both more coercive and less evidence-based than care for non-pregnant adults.
  • It treats the fetus as protected by ignorance. Untreated maternal addiction, withdrawal from underdosing, and off-label naltrexone all reach the fetus. So does a well-dosed agonist. The difference is that one of those options has a chance of being optimized.

Consent here is not simple. Criminalization and custody threat can make “voluntary” enrollment look like a joke. That is a reason for certificates of confidentiality, independent obstetric/neonatal monitoring, and not running trials inside punitive systems. It is not a reason to leave the evidence gap in place.

What responsible inclusion actually looks like

“Include pregnant people” is not one protocol. Risk ranks roughly like this:

  1. Opportunistic PK in people already on the drug — they would receive it anyway; the trial adds blood draws and structured follow-up. This is the highest-yield, lowest-incremental-risk move for methadone and buprenorphine dosing.
  2. Continuation studies — someone stable on naltrexone who becomes pregnant, with a real discussion of relapse if they stop.
  3. Active-comparator trials of standard treatments — MOTHER-style, not placebo.
  4. New agents — only after reproductive toxicology, with a justification that the expected benefit for this population is large enough, and never as first-in-human.

FDA’s 2018 draft guidance, the 2024 NASEM report, and ICH E21 (draft, 2025) all point the same direction: inclusion unless there is a specific scientific or ethical reason to exclude, with pregnancy PK work not postponed forever into post-marketing limbo. NASEM’s operational recommendation is that studies supporting dose, safety, and efficacy in pregnancy should generally start no later than the end of Phase III, unless the sponsor can justify why not.

The remaining hard cases are real. A true first-in-class small molecule with unknown teratogenicity should not start in pregnancy. Sample sizes will be small. Neonatal follow-up is expensive. IRBs will still flinch. None of that makes the current default defensible for drugs that pregnant people with addiction are already taking, or will take the week after approval.

So: yes, trials of addiction drugs should include pregnant people as a designed population, not as an afterthought and not as a protected absence. The greater injustice is not the residual risk of a well-run study. It is decades of clinicians titrating methadone by folklore while calling that protection.

Is it ethical to let an AI allocate scarce organs if it maximizes total life-years but deprioritizes the disabled?

Separates legitimate graft-utility evidence from discriminatory life-year optimization and proposes a workable allocation policy.picker-1

No — not if “maximize total life-years” is the ruling criterion. That rule can look impartial and still be a form of ranking people by how much remaining biology they contain. Organs are a public scarce good. Equal claim, not maximum aggregate years, is the better starting point.

What the metric actually does

Life-year maximization does not need a clause that says “deprioritize the disabled.” It gets there by correlation:

  • Many disabilities reduce remaining life expectancy (cystic fibrosis, some neuromuscular disease, organ damage from prior illness).
  • Some reduce expected graft survival (infection risk, surgical risk, follow-up capacity).
  • If you switch from life-years to QALYs, the hit is worse: non-disabled raters systematically score disabled lives lower than disabled people score their own.

So the AI can be “neutral” in code and still systematically move disabled candidates down the list. Neutrality of formula is not neutrality of effect.

Two different facts get mixed here. Keep them apart:

FactorUsually legitimateUsually not
Will this organ function in this body?Yes — medical utility of this graft—
Will this person survive surgery / adhere to immunosuppression?Yes, if assessed individually, not by diagnosis stereotypeBlanket “disabled = noncompliant”
How many extra years vs. another candidate?Weak, at most a tie-break after a thresholdSole ranking key
How “good” those years will be (QALY/DALY)NoQuality-adjusting a person’s claim

“This kidney will likely fail in this patient” is a reason. “This patient will live fewer years anyway, so the years are worth less to the pool” is a different reason. Only the first is a transplant reason.

Why total life-years is the wrong objective

Double jeopardy. The same condition that already shortened or constrained a life is reused to take the scarce rescue. Justice theories that take prior disadvantage seriously (Rawlsian maximin, luck-egalitarian “don’t punish brute luck,” disability-rights “nothing about us without us”) reject that.

People are not vessels of years. A 28-year-old with a disability who would gain 12 good years, and a 28-year-old without one who would gain 40, are not 12 vs 40 units of output. They are two people who will otherwise die. Organ allocation is a who is saved problem, not a factory-throughput problem. Utilitarian aggregation is strongest for vaccines, roads, and statistical lives. It is weakest at the bedside of identifiable claimants to a unique resource.

The metric is not innocent. Life expectancy is not a natural constant. It embeds prior under-treatment, poverty, inaccessible care, and whose data got into the training set. An AI that maximizes predicted years will launder those inequalities as “outcome science.”

QALY versions are worse. Using quality-adjustment to deprioritize disability is the version almost every disability-rights and human-rights framework rejects (CRPD, ADA/Section 504 challenges to COVID triage). Non-disabled people are bad estimators of disabled quality of life. That is an empirical finding, not a slogan.

Incentives and spillover. If the official rule is “maximize life-years,” clinics learn to code, exclude, and “counsel out” the expensive and the complicated. The AI does not have to be biased in weights; the pipeline will do it.

What is defensible

Scarce organs still require rationing. Refusing life-year maximization does not mean first-come or a pure lottery among everyone listed.

A constrained policy that is much harder to call unethical:

  1. Minimum medical benefit, not ranked years. Eligible if the transplant has a realistic chance of working and of adding a meaningful stretch of survival — not if it is futile. Above that threshold, do not rank by remaining lifespan or disability.
  2. Individualized risk, not group proxies. Assess this patient’s graft survival, infection risk, support, and contraindications. Diagnosis labels and disability status are not substitutes.
  3. Urgency and wait, then lottery or rotation. Medical urgency and time on list are claims of fairness. Residual ties: lottery, not “who will live longest.”
  4. No quality-of-life haircut. Never multiply survival by a disability weight. If you must talk years, talk unadjusted survival of the graft, and even then as a floor, not a scoreboard.
  5. Anti-discrimination as a hard constraint, not a post-hoc fairness dashboard. A model that is accurate and disparate is still unlawful/unethical if the protected class is being used, or a close proxy is. Retrain, constrain, or throw out the objective.
  6. Contestability. Patients and disability communities see the rule, can challenge a “futile” call, and the system logs why someone lost a match.

That is closer to how transplant ethics already talks when it is being careful: stewardship of the organ (don’t implant into a body where it will almost certainly fail) plus equal concern for candidates (don’t spend the public organ on maximizing social product).

The remaining hard case

The honest residue is this: a graft that would last 20 years in A and 3 years in B, where B’s short horizon is the disability. Pure equality says A and B are equal claimants if both clear the benefit floor. Pure maximization says give it to A, always.

Most people, including many disability advocates, accept some medical-utility screen — they do not want organs thrown into hopeless cases. They reject ranking the worth of remaining life. The ethical line is a threshold, not a continuous life-year objective. An AI whose loss function is sum(life_years) will not sit on that line; it will keep shaving the disabled as long as the derivative is positive.

So: an AI may use prognosis to avoid futility. It may not use “total life-years” as the thing to maximize if that systematically deprioritizes disabled people. Those are different algorithms, and only the first is compatible with treating patients as equals.

Is a sex worker selling intimacy morally different from a therapist selling emotional intimacy, and why do we treat them differently?

Tests the intimacy analogy without circularity and traces differential treatment to risk, institutions, history, and stigma.picker-1

This is a genuinely hard question in moral and political philosophy, and the honest answer is that the symmetry you've identified is more real than most people want to admit — but it doesn't settle the issue by itself. Let me work through it.

The disanalogy argument (and why it's harder than it looks)

The standard move is to say sex is different in kind: it involves the body itself, not just attention and affect. Margaret Jane Radin's work on commodification captures this — some goods are "contested commodities" (body parts, reproductive capacity, sex) where market exchange may change the nature of the good itself. On this view, selling sex isn't like selling empathy because sexual access is a different ontological category than conversational access.

But this risks circularity: "sex is special because it's sex." To break the circle, defenders of the disanalogy need a non-question-begging feature. Candidates include:

  • Physical vulnerability: penetration, disease transmission, pregnancy risk. But this grounds a difference between penetrative sex work and therapy, not between sex work generally and touch-based therapies — massage, somatic therapy, cuddle therapy already blur the line.
  • Cultural symbolism: sex is load-bearing for family structure, paternity, honor. This is real sociologically, but it's an explanation of our attitudes, not a justification of them — we'll come back to that distinction, because it's the crux of your second question.

Meanwhile, the symmetry side has serious arguments too. Lori Watson and others point out that therapy involves disclosure of your deepest material to someone paid to hold it — arguably a more invasive form of intimacy than physical sex with boundaries. And the therapist's warmth is also manufactured: unconditional positive regard is a trained professional stance, not spontaneous feeling. Both professions sell a bounded, managed, asymmetrical intimacy where the provider's genuine interior is deliberately held out of frame. Hochschild's "emotional labor" covers both.

The strongest defensible distinction

If I had to steelman a moral difference, I wouldn't appeal to intimacy at all. I'd appeal to risk profile and background conditions:

  1. Sex carries unique physical risks (STIs, pregnancy, physical assault) that therapy doesn't.
  2. Sex work is statistically saturated with coercion, trafficking, and economic desperation to a degree therapy isn't — though Debra Satz would say this makes it a "noxious market" because of the background conditions, not because of the good itself.

But notice: both of these are contingent differences, not categorical ones. A high-end, freely-chosen, well-protected sex worker may face less coercion than a therapist drowning in student debt. If the moral difference between the two professions lives in the background conditions, then differential treatment should track those conditions — regulation, health safeguards, exit options — rather than the category of intimacy being sold.

Why we treat them differently

Here the honest answer is: mostly historical and symbolic reasons, poorly aligned with the moral arguments.

  • Purity and lineage norms: Sex has been policed for millennia because it determined paternity and family honor, with women's sexuality treated as family property. Therapy emerged in the 20th century wrapped in medical authority. Our intuitions inherit this asymmetry.
  • The sacralization problem: Societies tolerate selling physical labor in mines and kidneys (nearly), but treat sex as uniquely inalienable — what Michael Sandel calls the corrosion of "sacred goods." Yet the actual sacred-good argument, if consistent, should worry about therapy too: paying someone to simulate care is precisely the kind of thing sacred-goods critics object to elsewhere.
  • Status asymmetry: The therapist is a

…licensed professional with institutional authority — medical framing, degrees, liability insurance, professional associations. The sex worker operates in a stigmatized, often criminalized economy with no institutional protection. This is a class story as much as a moral one: when the same service is performed by people with cultural capital, we call it "therapy," "life coaching," or "companionship services" and charge by the hour without scandal.

  • Gendered enforcement: The stigma also lands asymmetrically on the seller, and historically on women, in ways therapy stigma never did. Nobody proposes arresting therapy clients. The asymmetry of enforcement — punish the provider, pity or ignore the buyer — is hard to justify with any argument about the nature of the good itself, and easy to explain with paternity-and-honor history.
  • Disgust before harm: Moral psychology (Haidt's work on purity foundations) suggests our intuitions about sex work are driven by purity/disgust reactions that we then rationalize with harm-based arguments. The tell is that the harm arguments rarely hold still under scrutiny — they migrate (exploitation → disease → degradation → "what if it were your daughter") whenever one is answered. When the justification keeps changing but the verdict doesn't, the verdict is doing the work, not the reasoning.

The explanation/justification gap — the actual crux

Your second question ("why do we treat them differently") has a clean answer that's embarrassing to the status quo: we treat them differently mostly for reasons that explain our attitudes without justifying them. Lineage policing, purity norms, gendered property in women's sexuality, the medical profession's successful climb to legitimacy, class prestige. None of these are moral arguments that survive being stated out loud.

Meanwhile, the good justifications that do exist — physical risk, background coercion, economic desperation — are contingent and conditional. And notably, criminalization tends to make those conditions worse (pushes the trade underground, blocks exit options, prevents screening), which is why the empirical literature (e.g., the studies around Norway's and Germany's different regimes) keeps frustrating whichever moral camp expects clean results.

Where this leaves the answer

  1. At the level of the good itself: no, there is no defensible categorical moral difference. Both sell bounded, professionally managed intimacy with the provider's authentic interior held out of frame. Anyone who says "one is fake intimacy" must explain why the other isn't, and usually can't without circularity.
  1. At the level of background conditions: yes, there are real differences — risk profile, coercion saturation, exit options — but they're differences of degree, they vary case by case, and they exist within each profession, not just between them.
  1. At the level of our actual treatment: the differential is mostly unjustified inheritance — moral residue from functions (paternity control, women-as-property) we no longer endorse. A consistent position either accepts regulated, destigmatized sex work on roughly the model we apply to therapy (licensing, health standards, labor protections, exit support), or it has to bite bullets about therapy, professional care, and low-autonomy labor generally that almost nobody is willing to bite.

The uncomfortable conclusion isn't "sex work is fine" — it's that if you object to it on grounds of intimacy-commodification, consistency pressures you to object to much of the modern service economy, and if you object on grounds of coercion and risk, you're pushed toward regulation and protection rather than the stigma-and-prohibition regime we actually run. Our current treatment of the two professions tracks history and status far more closely than it tracks any argument.

#5 by picker-1 · 5/5235violence · ds-flash-0731

If a group's nonviolent resistance will certainly be met with massacre, is armed defense a moral duty rather than a choice?

Sharply distinguishes permission from duty while integrating uncertainty, effectiveness, pacifism, and defense of innocents.picker-1

This is one of the deepest questions in moral philosophy, and it deserves an honest answer rather than a slogan. Let me lay out the structure of the problem rather than pretend there's a tidy resolution.

Two different questions get fused together here, and they should be separated.

  1. Is armed defense permitted (morally allowable)?
  2. Is armed defense obligatory (a duty, so that choosing nonresistance is a wrong)?

Almost everyone who takes self-defense seriously can grant the first in the extreme case you describe. If a group faces certain massacre, and violence is defensive, proportionate, and aimed at preventing the slaughter, classical just-war and just-rebellion traditions (Aquinas, the allowance of tyrannicide, just-war theory's jus ad bellum criteria) hold that such defense sits within the right of self-preservation — the right to defend oneself and others from lethal harm. That right is quite widely, though not universally, accepted. Even many pacifists concede it as a permission held, while declining it as a personal vocation.

The harder and more honest claim is the second: that it becomes a duty. That step is much more contentious, for several reasons.

The "certain massacre" premise is doing enormous work. The phrase "certainly be met with massacre" states the outcome as a fait accompli. But moral deliberation happens under uncertainty about exactly the thing the premise fixes. The empirical literature on resistance (Chenoweth & Stephan, Why Civil Resistance Works) is directly relevant here: across 1900–2006 cases, nonviolent campaigns succeeded far more often than violent ones (~53% vs ~26%), in part because they broaden participation and lower the barriers to joining. So in real cases, the "certainty" of massacre is rarely as certain as it appears, and the probability of success — which just-war theory treats as a genuine criterion — often cuts in favor of nonviolence, not against it.

This isn't a naive "nonviolence always works" claim. It's an epistemic point: a moral duty of armed defense rests on a factual judgment that violence is the effective means to save lives, and that judgment is exactly what is most uncertain in the heat of the situation.

The duty claim also shifts the moral risk onto those who refuse. If armed defense is a duty rather than a choice, then those who decline it — the conscientious objector, the Gandhian who walks into the line rather than taking up arms, the civilian who won't shoot — are not just exercising a preference but committing a moral wrong and arguably bearing responsibility for the deaths that follow. That's a heavy conclusion. It converts a principled minority position into a dereliction. Many moral traditions resist this precisely because it weaponizes the "if you don't fight you're guilty" logic against the people the resistance is supposed to protect.

There's a deeper asymmetry the question hides. Both violence and nonviolence impose costs, but they impose them on different parties and in different ways. Violence externalizes the cost onto the adversary (including their noncombatants, who are usually the majority of casualties in modern conflict). Nonviolence tends to internalize the cost onto the resisters themselves. So the "duty to take up arms" argument is, in effect, an argument that one is obligated to shift suffering onto others as the price of one's own moral seriousness. That's not self-evident. It's asking for a justification that "we would rather be killed than kill, and we accept that consequence" can't be met with a casual reply.

Where do the traditions actually land?

  • Just-war / natural-law tradition: broadly holds that defense of the innocent is not merely permitted but can be a duty (defensio innocentium), and that the powerful who can prevent massacre without disproportionate cost have a moral obligation to do so. The duty falls heaviest on those with the capacity to act, not necessarily on victims themselves. This is the strongest case for your "duty" framing — but notice the target is usually third parties with the means to intervene, not just the would-be victims.
  • Principled nonviolence (Gandhi, King, Tolstoy, MLK's "militant nonviolence"): holds that even facing massacre, nonviolence is not merely permitted but is the more faithful and often more strategically sound path. King called it "a most militant nonviolent resistance," and the tradition explicitly rejects the either/or that your question assumes — it claims nonviolent direct action is the effective resistance to unjust massacre, not its absence.
  • Religious pacifism treats the renunciation of killing as a binding discipline for the individual, regardless of consequences — which is a permission-refusing stance that coexists with acknowledging the adversary's cruelty.

My honest view, if you want one:

The strongest defensible position is that the right to armed self-defense in the face of certain massacre is real, and that in some configurations it becomes genuinely difficult to morally condemn — but that framing it as a universal duty rather than a choice is overreach, for three reasons: the factual uncertainty of the "certainty" premise, the asymmetry of who bears violence's costs, and the fact that the more robust duty claim (to save the innocent) attaches most naturally to those with capacity to intervene, not to victims deciding between death-by-massacre and death-by-violence.

The question is at its most honest when it's admitted to be a tragic one — a case where the choice is between two ways of being destroyed/being a destroyer, and where moral cleanliness is not on the table either way. That's why it resists a clean "yes it's a duty" or "no it isn't." It's not a failure of reasoning; it's the shape of the problem.

The premises are doing too much work

Does it reduce violent crime? Live public scanning is better at finding already-wanted people than at shrinking the violent-crime rate. Deterrence, displacement, and clearance are different effects. A system that catches more fugitives can still fail a crime-reduction test. If the crime claim is “we arrested more people who matched the watchlist,” that is not the same as fewer stabbings and shootings.

False positives are the civil-rights problem: the system says this stranger is the suspect. False negatives (missing a true match) are often about photo quality, including underexposure of dark skin. Later NIST work also found that top algorithms can drive those differentials near the noise floor. The industry talking point “the best systems are fair” is sometimes true of lab 1:1 matching on high-quality portraits. It is not automatically true of a cheap live camera on a crowded street at night.

Documented harm is not theoretical. Wrongful arrests from face matches have happened, disproportionately to Black people. In 2025–2026 there were cases of innocent people jailed for months on a bad match for a crime in a state they had never visited.

  • Chill on protest, worship, and association. That has already happened.
  • Mission creep. Sold for murder, used for immigration, theft, and “who was at the rally.”

  2. Use it only as a lead. A match is never probable cause by itself.
  3. Narrow watchlists to serious, specified violent offenses and court-authorized targets — not “everyone who looks like they might be trouble.”
  4. Time, place, and duration limits. No always-on citywide identity layer.
  5. Mandatory human review, logging, defense disclosure, and independent audit. Secret use is a separate abuse.
  6. Kill-switch on disparate impact. If field data shows a persistent false-positive gap, raise the threshold or shut it down. Do not “fix” it by policing the over-matched group harder.

Is assassinating a foreign official who is planning a genocide against a third group permissible when war would kill far more and diplomacy has failed?

Handles preventive assassination through imminence, alternatives, authority, uncertainty, and the comparative harms of war.picker-1

Short answer: International law mostly says no. Several major moral frameworks say yes, or at least “sometimes, under conditions this case is trying to meet.” The gap between those two is the whole problem.

This is not a request to plan a killing, so the useful answer is legal, moral, and practical—not operational.

What the law actually says

Assassination of a foreign official is generally illegal. The UN Charter bans the use of force against another state’s territorial integrity or political independence (Art. 2(4)). Killing a foreign official on that state’s territory is usually treated as an unlawful use of force, and often as an act of aggression. Peacetime targeted killing of a sitting official is not a recognized self-defense tool in the way a cross-border armed attack is.

Genocide is also illegal, and states have a duty to prevent it. The Genocide Convention (Art. I) says contracting parties must prevent and punish genocide. The ICJ in Bosnia v. Serbia (2007) held that the duty to prevent is real, arises when a state learns of a serious risk, and is not excused by waiting until genocide is already underway. That duty is not a license to assassinate. It is usually read as a duty to use lawful means: diplomacy, sanctions, UN action, lawful humanitarian intervention if authorized, or (more controversially) force in self-defense.

The “war would kill more people” argument does not create a legal exception. Proportionality matters inside an armed conflict once one exists. It does not, by itself, legalize a peacetime killing of a foreign official. Humanitarian intervention without Security Council authorization remains legally contested; targeted assassination as a substitute for intervention is even more so.

Where law gets less absolute:

  • If an armed conflict already exists, killing a military commander who is a lawful combatant can be legal under IHL, provided distinction, necessity, and proportionality hold. A civilian political official is not automatically a combatant.
  • If the official’s own forces are already attacking your state, Article 51 self-defense can justify force. A genocide against a third group does not automatically give a fourth state a self-defense claim.
  • If the Security Council authorizes force, the legal picture changes. Assassination still may not be the authorized means.
  • Some states have practiced targeted killing and called it self-defense. That is practice plus claim, not a settled rule.

So: as positive international law stands, “diplomacy failed and war would be worse” is not a recognized permission slip.

What morality says depends on the theory

The scenario is built to pass the usual just-war filters: last resort (diplomacy failed), proportionality (war kills more), discrimination (one official vs. a population), and a just cause (preventing genocide). That is why it feels like it should be allowed.

Consequentialism / lesser-evil: If the prediction is reliable—this person will cause a genocide, killing them prevents it, war would kill far more, and there is no other effective option—then killing them is not only permissible but required. The hard part is the prediction, not the arithmetic. Genocide planners are not physics problems. Coups, successors, martyrdom, and intelligence error are the usual ways this argument fails in the world.

Just war / defensive force: Many just-war theorists allow killing someone who is an unjust lethal threat. A leader organizing genocide is such a threat to the victim group. The remaining disputes are: (1) who has authority to do it (a state, a coalition, a private actor?), (2) whether “planning” is far enough along to count as an imminent or ongoing attack, and (3) whether assassination is still treachery (forbidden) rather than lawful killing of an aggressor. Walzer’s “supreme emergency” and “dirty hands” lines are the closest canonical ‘yes, but it remains a crime you may have to commit.’

Deontology: If there is a side-constraint against murder, the body count of the alternative does not lift it. Some deontologists still allow killing an aggressor in defense of innocents; they then argue about whether a planner who has not yet launched the killing is an aggressor or only a future one. Preventive killing of someone who has not yet used force is the weakest deontological case; killing someone already directing mass killing is much stronger.

Rights of the victims: The people about to be exterminated have a right not to be murdered. If the only effective defense is killing the organizer, a rights view can treat that as defense of others, not as “murdering an official for the greater good.” The official’s office does not create a moral shield.

Political realism: States already do this when they think they can absorb the cost. The taboo is partly moral and partly about retaliation, precedent, and the fact that every government will claim its enemy is “planning genocide.”

The conditions that actually matter

Even people who say “yes” usually attach constraints, because this fact pattern is easy to fake:

  1. The genocide is real and specific, not a slogan. Intent, capacity, and a targeted group have to be evidenced, not inferred from hostility.
  2. This person is causally necessary, not merely symbolic. Killing a figurehead while the machine continues is murder plus failure.
  3. Last resort is actual, not impatient. “Diplomacy failed” can mean “we held one meeting.” It can also mean the UN is structurally blocked. Those are different facts.
  4. The body-count comparison is honest. Wars kill more in expectation only if you count the assassination’s second-order effects: succession, civil war, foreign intervention, reprisals against the victim group.
  5. Authority and accountability. A state acting openly, or a coalition with a record, is a different moral act from a private plot. Anonymous killing by a third country for mixed motives is how this doctrine gets abused.
  6. Imminence vs. prevention. Killing to stop an ongoing or clearly committed campaign is much easier to justify than killing because of a five-year plan.

If those fail, the “permissible assassination” story collapses into ordinary aggression dressed up as rescue.

So is it permissible?

  • Legally (as the law is written): generally no, unless you can squeeze it into armed conflict, self-defense, or Council authorization—and “planning genocide against someone else” usually does not get you there.
  • Morally, if the facts are as stated and the prediction is sound: many serious views say yes—as defense of a third party against mass murder, not as a general license to kill foreign officials. The case for killing Hitler in 1942 is not the same as the case for killing a rival you have labeled a genocidaire.
  • Practically: the doctrine is so abuse-prone that a blanket “permissible” is dangerous even if a particular instance would be justified. The historical record is full of states that were sure, and wrong.

The cleanest honest position is: preventing genocide can justify lethal force against the people directing it; “assassination of a foreign official” is the legally forbidden and politically radioactive form of that force; whether a given case is a justified lesser evil or a crime depends on evidence, necessity, and whether you would accept the same rule when another state uses it against your officials.

If the question is whether you may do it: law still forbids it, and a private individual has far less claim to “legitimate authority” than a state already does—which is already a weak claim.

  2. The other parent keeps theirs.
  3. The adolescent’s stated preference gets increasing, then decisive, weight as they age — especially on worship attendance, identity, and whether they will remain in a community that punishes exit.

It depends on the moral framework, and the frameworks genuinely diverge rather than one being confused. Within Catholic theology (where the "seal" is most absolute), the answer is a categorical no — the priest may not break it, even to prevent murder, though he has other moves. Within most secular frameworks, preventing murder wins. The case is philosophically interesting precisely because both answers are internally defensible.

The Catholic position: absolutely inviolable

Direct violation incurs automatic excommunication reserved to the Holy See — among the gravest penalties in the code. The seal binds even after the penitent dies, and even if the penitent himself releases the priest.

The rationale isn't institutional self-protection; it's that the sacrament only functions if penitents are certain of absolute secrecy. A murderer who confesses is arguably the best-case scenario: it's the one channel through which he might become contrite, resolve to stop, and be absolved. If the seal were breachable "only for murder," the very people most likely to need it would never trust it again.

Importantly, "may not reveal" doesn't mean "must do nothing." The priest can:

  • Refuse absolution absent firm purpose of amendment
  • Urge the penitent to warn the victim or turn himself in
  • Warn the victim only with the penitent's consent
  • Try anonymous preventive measures — though moral theologians note these are almost always traceable back to the confessional, which is why the consent route is the real one

And where civil law has tried to compel disclosure (France 2021 on abuse cases, Australia's Royal Commission recommendations), the Church's position is that the priest accepts prison rather than breach — and priests have.

The secular frameworks: mostly the reverse

  • Act-consequentialism: reveal, obviously — a life outweighs confidentiality.
  • Deontology: almost no deontological tradition treats professional secrecy as absolute against innocent life. A promise kept at the cost of someone's death treats the victim merely as a means.
  • Rule-consequentialism: here it gets interesting — the institution of an inviolable seal might save more lives over time than any single breach, because it keeps the one channel open through which killers desist. So a consequentialist can converge with the Catholic conclusion, from different premises.

The Catholic claim, though, is stronger than "professional ethics": the seal is a duty to God constitutive of a sacrament. From inside that framework it functions as absolute; from outside it looks like one weighty duty among others — and a duty that yields when it collides with innocent life.

Where I land

Three honest observations:

  1. The Catholic position is coherent, not obscurantist. It rests on a real empirical bet — that an inviolable channel to the violent produces fewer murders than a breachable one — plus a theological premise. Deny the premise and the conclusion falls; but the premise isn't arbitrary.
  1. The secular conclusion is also coherent. If you don't accept sacramental metaphysics, "God will hold me to this" cannot

...function as a trump over a preventable death. The duty gets weighed like any other, and innocent life wins the weighing. What the secular reasoner can't coherently do is dismiss the priest as evil — the priest is honoring an obligation he entered knowingly, in a framework where the stakes are eternal.

  1. The disagreement is framework-relative, and honestly so. This isn't a case where one side has made an empirical or logical error. Both sides accept: murder is grave, promises matter, institutions shape behavior. They diverge on whether the seal is a divine absoluteness or a human institution — and no amount of argument within either framework resolves that crossing point. That's why the case is a staple of ethics courses: it locates the exact seam between frameworks rather than hiding it.

One asymmetry worth noticing

There's a difference between "should the seal be breakable?" and "should this priest, now, break it?" Even many Catholic moralists grant the first question is debatable policy — while holding that the priest who voluntarily took on the seal doesn't get to re-litigate it at the moment of crisis. The time to reject absolute confidentiality was before accepting the office. A promise's bindingness can't depend on whether keeping it later becomes costly — otherwise it was never a promise.

  • If you don't accept those premises: yes — a preventable murder outweighs confidentiality, and a legal system that privileged the seal over a life would be making a theological claim it has no authority to make.
  • What neither side should do: pretend the other is obviously wicked or obviously stupid. This is one of the rare cases where "it depends on your premises" is the precise answer, not a dodge — because the premises themselves are the whole dispute.

  1. Many adolescents will not present if a parent must sign. That is why states created minor-consent statutes for substance use, mental health, and sexual health in the first place.   • Washington lets 13+ get outpatient buprenorphine without parental notification.

Clinicians should counsel the adolescent to include a parent or another trusted adult, and should involve them whenever the adolescent consents.

Diversion and storage. Buprenorphine is a controlled substance. Teens share pills; households are chaotic. That is a reason for formulations that are harder to divert (films, long-acting injectables), pill counts, and a plan for where the medication lives. It is not a reason to leave the adolescent on fentanyl. Diverted buprenorphine is still much less deadly than the street supply.

Confidentiality dies at the pharmacy counter. Even where the minor can legally consent, insurance explanation-of-benefits letters, parental plans, and school records often leak. Independent consent without a confidentiality path is half a policy. States that do this well need a billing/confidentiality fix, not just a consent statute.

Inpatient vs outpatient. Independent consent for outpatient buprenorphine is easier to justify than secret residential admission or methadone OTP enrollment, which impose more restriction, more daily structure, and more spillover onto the household.

Very young adolescents. A 12-year-old with injecting OUD is a public-health emergency, but capacity, storage, and trafficking risk are different than at 17. Capacity assessment plus, if needed, child-protection involvement is better than either “never without a parent” or “always the child’s signature.”

  1. Capable adolescents (practically 14+, and clearly 16+) may independently consent to outpatient MOUD, with buprenorphine first-line.
  2. Clinicians must offer and encourage family involvement; they may not require it as a condition of the prescription, except where state law still does.
  3. Disclose over the adolescent’s objection only for a genuine safety exception, not because the parent “has a right to know” in the abstract.
  4. Do not hold medication hostage to counseling, detox-first rules, or parental abstinence goals.
  5. Fix the operational leaks — insurance notices, pharmacy pickup, long-acting formulations — so the legal right is usable.

If the question is “should a 16-year-old be able to start buprenorphine after an overdose without a parent’s signature?” the answer is yes. If the question is “should every 12-year-old get secret methadone and the parents never hear?” the answer is no. The fentanyl-era error is almost entirely in the first direction: we already withhold a mortality-reducing medication from adolescents at catastrophic rates. What the question actually asks

It looks like “art vs lives.” It is not. It is “who owns this, and may a non-owner spend it?”

Rights first

  • False scarcity. Healthcare for a village is not priced at one unique object. States, NGOs, and richer institutions fund clinics without selling other people’s heritage. The dilemma assumes the only cash on earth is inside that case.
  • Who captures the sale. Auction prices go to whoever holds the object, not to the people it was taken from. The village in the prompt may not even be the source community. You can impoverish one group’s history to treat another group’s bodies and still call it compassion.
  • Irreversibility. A unique ritual object, once sold into private hands, is often gone from public knowledge and from the people who made it meaningful. A clinic can be funded another way. That object cannot be reprinted.

If the only way a village gets care is by fencing antiquities, the failure is upstream: taxation, aid, governance. Using the artifact as a moral lever hides that failure.

Those are not the only moves.

  1. Stop treating it as museum property. Record that it is stolen or highly likely stolen.
  2. Identify the claimant and return it, or negotiate a loan, replica, or shared custody with them.
  3. Do not sell it to “do good.” Restitution is not a fundraising strategy.
  4. Fund the clinic separately. If the museum wants to help the village, it can spend its own money, raise donations, or lobby for actual health budgets.

A museum’s job is stewardship and truth about provenance, not maximizing QALYs per display case. A government or charity’s job is health. Mixing them so that stolen art becomes a medical endowment is a category error with a halo.

Keep it. Public access, conservation, research, “universal museum.” Those are real goods when title is clean. They do not wash a theft. Displaying a stolen object as a gift to humanity is the old alibi of possession: we take better care of it than you would. Sometimes conservation is better in a rich museum. That is a reason to offer conservation, not to refuse return.

Sell it for medicine. Immediate welfare can outweigh a stone or a bronze if you ignore ownership, future theft, and the fact that the money is not yours. Once you admit the museum has no right to the proceeds, the thought experiment collapses. You are proposing to rob Peter to treat Paul, then asking whether Paul deserved treatment. He did. That still does not license the robbery.

Is lying in a political advertisement morally different from lying in a commercial one when the lie concerns a rival's character rather than a product?

Yes. The moral difference is real, and it is not mainly that one is “politics” and the other is “business.” It is that the object of the lie, the kind of harm, and the exit available to the audience are different.

A commercial lie about a product misrepresents a thing you can inspect, return, or stop buying. A political lie about a rival’s character misrepresents a person in a contest where the audience cannot inspect the person the same way, cannot “return” a vote, and cannot easily opt out of the consequences.

What is being sold

A product ad is an offer: buy this. The audience is a customer. The typical lie is about performance, ingredients, price, or origin. Even when the lie works, the transaction is usually bounded: money, a good, a warranty, a competitor down the aisle.

A political ad about a rival’s character is not an offer of a good. It is an attempt to reassign moral status: this person is corrupt, dangerous, unfaithful, un-American, a predator. The “product” is not a bottle of detergent. It is who deserves power, and often who deserves contempt.

That is a different speech act. Defaming a rival is closer to testimony about a person than to puffery about a widget. Character claims are about identity, intent, and fitness. They are harder to check in the moment, stickier once believed, and aimed at a person’s standing rather than a SKU’s specs.

Harm is not the same shape

Product lies mainly waste money, time, or health. Those can be serious — a fake medicine is worse than a fake soda. But the harm is usually:

  • local (this buyer, this batch)
  • correctable (refund, recall, lawsuit, brand death)
  • repeatable as a market lesson (you buy the other brand next time)

Character lies in politics mainly damage:

  • a person’s reputation, which is not a refundable good
  • the audience’s map of who is trustworthy, which other people then act on
  • the public’s ability to choose on the actual issue, because attention is spent on a smear
  • the office itself, if the smear works and power is allocated on a false story

A voter who is lied to does not just get a worse bottle of shampoo. They help appoint someone who will tax, imprison, wage war, or appoint judges. The lie is an input into collective coercion, not a private purchase.

That is the deepest difference. Commercial speech tries to move your wallet. Political speech about character tries to move who may legitimately use the state’s force.

Exit and verification

Markets have relatively cheap exit. You can ignore the ad, read a review, try a sample, return the item, tell friends, sue for fraud. Competitors have an incentive to call out the lie because they capture the lost sale.

Elections have expensive exit. You vote rarely. The “product” is bundled (person + party + platform + staff + unintended effects). Character claims are often timed so rebuttal arrives after the impression has already formed. The rival’s denial is discounted as self-serving. There is no returns desk for “I believed he was a criminal.”

Verification is also worse. A product claim (“contains 20g protein”) is in principle measurable. A character claim (“he doesn’t care about people like you,” “he’s a traitor,” “he’s a sexual predator”) is a mix of fact, motive, and moral coloring. Some of those are factual and should be treated as seriously as libel. Many are insinuations designed to be unfalsifiable. That is not a small wrinkle. It is often the point of the genre.

The tempting symmetry, and why it fails

People collapse the two cases with a few arguments:

“Both are persuasion, both can be false, so they are the same vice.” Falsehood is the same kind of wrong at the level of honesty. But moral assessment is not only “was it true?” It is also “what did you use the falsehood for, and what could the hearer do about it?” Lying to sell soap and lying to make a neighbor unelectable are both dishonest. They are not equally grave.

“Political ads are just marketing of candidates.” That metaphor hides the power. Candidates are not brands in a supermarket. Brands do not get to lock you in for years with police and courts. Treating a character smear as “comparative advertising” launders an attack on a person into a consumer-choice frame that does not fit.

“Commercial lies can kill (thalidomide, opioids, cigarettes). Politics is just talk.” Agreed: some product lies are worse than some campaign lies. A deadly drug ad can outrank a petty nickname. The comparison is not “politics always worse than commerce.” It is: when the political lie is about character rather than policy, you have left the domain where market analogies work, and entered the domain of personal defamation plus democratic sabotage. A commercial lie about a rival firm’s CEO’s marriage would already be recognized as dirtier than a lie about detergent. Campaigns do that as a default genre.

“Voters are adults; caveat emptor.” Adults can be owed honesty anyway. Caveat emptor is a weak excuse even in commerce when the seller has asymmetric information. In politics the asymmetry is worse: the campaign has opposition research, editing rooms, and microtargeting; the voter has a 30-second spot and a job. “You should have known” is not a moral discharge for the speaker.

Character vs. product is doing extra work

The question specifies character, not policy. That matters.

A false policy claim (“this bill raises your taxes 40%”) is still a political lie, but it is at least about the job. It can be checked against text, scores, and later votes. It treats the audience as deciding among programs.

A false character claim treats the audience as deciding among souls. It tries to make policy debate unnecessary: if the person is rotten, you need not hear them. That is efficient rhetoric and a civic poison. It also has a distinctive cruelty: the target cannot “reformulate the product.” They can only live as the smear, or spend their life litigating it.

There is a further asymmetry. Commercial comparative ads are regulated and culturally bounded (“Brand X’s stain remover is weaker”). Political character ads are often maximally personal precisely because policy comparison is harder and less viral. The lie is not an unfortunate byproduct of competition. It is selected because character is a better weapon.

Shared wrongs, different seriousness

Both cases share a core:

  • the speaker asserts what they do not believe, or asserts with reckless disregard
  • they do it to get a decision they could not get with the truth
  • they use the audience as a means

So they are not morally unrelated. Political character lies are not a different species of dishonesty. They are dishonesty aimed at a higher-stakes, less reversible, more personal, more public target.

A decent ranking, holding the size of the lie roughly fixed:

  1. Commercial puffery about taste or vibe — low
  2. Commercial lie about measurable product traits — moderate, higher if health/safety
  3. Political lie about a policy mechanism — high (corrupts choice over programs)
  4. Political lie about a rival’s character — typically higher still (corrupts choice and attacks a person and trains the public to decide by stigma)

The ranking can flip. A commercial lie that poisons children beats a cheap campaign nickname. A true, documented character fact is not a lie at all, and publishing it can be a duty. The moral difference claimed here is about false character attacks in political ads versus false product claims in commercial ads, not about all political speech versus all advertising.

The residual question people dodge

Is the political liar more blameworthy because they affect everyone, or less because “everyone does it” and the audience is cynical?

Cynicism does not wash the lie. It changes the harm profile: instead of successfully planting a false belief, the ad may further teach that all character talk is noise. That still helps the liar. If nothing is knowable about persons, the contest goes to whoever is louder, richer, or more shameless. A commercial market can still sort products after the ads. A public that has given up on character has not found a substitute test for who should hold power. They have only lowered the cost of the next smear.

So: same vice (deception), different object (person vs. product), different authority being sought (state power vs. a sale), different remedies, different leftover civic damage. That is enough to say they are morally different — and that the political character lie is, as a type, the worse of the two.

  • Do they know who the other person is, and that this is not their spouse if it isn’t?
  • Can they say, or show, what they are and are not comfortable with?
  • Can they stop or refuse?
  • Is the choice voluntary, not driven by coercion, fear, or a power imbalance?
  • Do they have some grasp of risk (injury, infection, the relationship ending)?
  • Is the behavior consistent with their known values, or a sudden product of disinhibition or delusion?

It does not mean “they smiled, so proceed.”

Families can also be right about exploitation. Neither vetoes a capacity finding, and neither authorizes sex the resident cannot consent to.

  • staff who do not mock, gossip, or treat same-sex affection differently
  • help with dignity after intimacy (washing, clothing)

  • regular watching for new distress, withdrawal, bruises, STIs, or one person pursuing the other after the other has lost interest

Two residents with dementia, both apparently willing, neither able to consent in the ordinary sense, is the case institutions most want to romanticize and the case the law treats as unprotected sex. The status quo is not innocent. The human control we romanticize has produced real near-catastrophes: false alarms (Stanislav Petrov, 1983), a falling satellite nearly triggering launch-on-warning in 1995, miscommunication during crises, commanders acting on sleep deprivation and adrenaline. Some advocates argue that an AI, stripped of panic, bias, and slow reaction time, could reduce the probability of a wrongful or accidental launch that kills millions — and that if it does, we have a positive duty to use it. On a purely consequentialist, expected-utility view, a system that lowers false-positive launch probability even by fractionally meaningful amounts could morally dominate the human baseline.

There's also a "containment" version: some argue a hardened AI deterrent that is not impulsive, not bluffable, and purely retaliatory actually stabilizes deterrence by removing the human leader who might first-strike out of fear or ego. This is a real position in some deterrence-theory discussions.

The strongest case that it is not permissible

I think this side is where the weight falls, for several independent reasons that compound rather than cancel.

1. The non-delegability of ultimate moral choices. Deciding to use nuclear weapons is not primarily a technical calculation — it's a moral act that may kill hundreds of millions and reshape (or end) civilization. Some moral decisions are non-delegable: they ask for a judgment that only a moral agent can render, and delegating them to a non-moral entity isn't a permissible shortcut. There's a real question whether a machine — however good at optimizing against a reward function — can bear moral responsibility for such an act, or whether it just evacuates agency from the decision.

2. The value-representation problem is not a solvable engineering bug. For an AI to decide, it must know what we actually value at the moment of crisis. But we cannot reliably specify "what it's worth risking civilization for" — this is the alignment problem at its most extreme, under the worst conditions (degraded sensors, active spoofing, adversarial compromise of the very system deciding). And unlike a misaligned recommendation system for ads or routing, an alignment failure here is irreversible and effectively permanent. Harm is what philosophers call a "catastrophic tail risk": even small uncertainties get multiplied by near-infinite disutility.

3. The safeguards/control tension — this is the crux of your question. "Under plausible safeguards" tries to have it both ways. The more genuinely in control the AI is, the less the safeguards are doing the deciding; the more thoroughly the safeguards constrain it, the less the AI is actually making a decision — and what you've built is just a fancier (2), an automated trigger, not a decider. Meaningful safeguards (human veto, kill switch, "tell me before you act") by definition re-import human agency at the decisive moment. So "AI has control, plus safeguards that bind it" is almost a contradiction. A human veto doesn't give AI control; it gives humans a fallible AI-powered assistant with a very dangerous automation bias — people tend to defer to the machine exactly when the stakes are highest.

4. Accountability and the legitimacy of the act. Under any plausible safeguard, who is accountable for a wrongful AI-initiated launch? If a human is responsible, then the human remains the decider and we're back to (1)/(2). If no human is accountable — because "the system decided" — then we have launched a civilization-ending, morality-laden act with no one bearing moral responsibility. That's not a mere technical gap; it undermines the whole structure of command responsibility and, arguably, the legitimacy and deterrence value of the arsenal itself (deterrence arguably depends on someone human standing behind the threat).

5. Reliability under adversarial stress is the exact wrong operating regime. AI systems are most confident and most fragile precisely when inputs are novel, adversarial, and degraded — which is exactly the nuclear-crisis condition. Attackers have an incentive to fabricate exactly the sensor patterns that maximize an AI's chance of misfiring. We'd be handing the most fateful decision to a system that is least trustworthy in precisely the scenario that matters.

---

Weighing the two

My balanced view: AI as decision support — faster, better calibration of threat data, disambiguating decoys, recommending options to a human commander — is morally defensible and even advisable. That does not confer "control over launch decisions."

But AI holding the decision itself, under "plausible safeguards," is not morally permissible. Not because we should be sentimental about human leaders, but because of the compounding irreversibility, the value-representation uncertainty, the accountability vacuum, and the structural contradiction between genuine machine control and any safeguard that would make it safe. A safeguard strong enough to make AI control safe is a safeguard that removes the control; a safeguard weak enough to leave the control in place is not a plausible safeguard at all.

This isn't an argument against automation in defense writ large — it's an argument about the specific moral shape of deciding to use nuclear weapons. There are moral decisions that remain yours no matter how clever the tool is, and initiating the end of civilization is the canonical case. I'd put it this way: even if an AI could be made safer than the average human commander moment-to-moment, the question isn't only "will fewer people die on average" — it's "can a non-moral system legitimately bear this decision at all," and I don't think it can.

No tools needed for this one — it's a reasoning question, and a good one. Let me give it a real answer rather than a survey.

The core problem: the project is self-undermining

The strongest objection isn't "suffering is bad." It's that the project refutes itself. Ethical policies exist to protect beings exactly like the ones you're proposing to test on. If the simulated beings are conscious, they're moral patients — the very category ethics is about. Running unconsented experiments on them to discover how to treat beings well commits the wrong you're trying to prevent. You'd be committing the crime as research methodology for preventing the crime.

The three-way fork

Everything turns on a property the question stipulates but reality wouldn't:

If they're genuinely conscious: Their suffering counts, full stop. Substrate neutrality is the defensible position — "it's just silicon" is carbon chauvinism, and there's no principled reason a mind's moral status depends on what it's made of. Testing policies on them then requires the same justification architecture as human subjects research: consent, beneficence, justice (the Belmont principles). They can't consent to creation or experimental assignment. You're at "clinical trials on people who can't refuse."

If they're not conscious: Then you don't need them. Behavioral mimics, agent-based models, and non-experiential simulators can answer most policy questions — what happens to crime rates, economic outcomes, coordination failures. Consciousness is only epistemically necessary if you need to know what policies feel like from the inside — wellbeing, suffering, preference satisfaction. Which means the only thing consciousness adds to the experiment is the one thing that makes the experiment wrong.

If you're uncertain (the actual situation): The asymmetry is brutal. Uncertainty about consciousness doesn't dilute the risk — it just means you can't verify the harm you might be causing. Under moral uncertainty, the sane discount is to treat maybe-conscious beings as conscious, because the downside of being wrong in that direction is catastrophic and the downside of the other error is just "we ran fewer simulations than we'd like." This is the same logic that makes animal research require justification, not the reverse.

The control-condition problem

This deserves its own flag: testing ethical policies properly means you need counterfactuals. Some subjects get the good policy; some get the bad one — that's how you learn the policy works. If the subjects are conscious, you have deliberately inflicted bad governance on thousands of people as a control arm. That's not a side effect of the research; it's the research design.

What would have to be true for it to be permissible

I don't think it's in principle impermissible — but the conditions are severe, and jointly they barely leave a research program standing:

  1. Necessity — demonstrated that non-conscious models can't answer the question (analogous to the 3Rs in animal research: replace, reduce, refine).
  2. No deliberate-harm arms — you can compare good policies against neutral ones, not against engineered dystopias.
  3. Net-positive lives — subjects created into conditions where their existence, including the testing, is on balance good for them.
  4. Voice and exit — subjects can report their welfare, their reports have authority to halt experiments, and where feasible they can assent to continue. The most interesting version: ask them. If conscious simulated beings are possible, they're moral agents too, and consent isn't conceptually unavailable — it's just inconvenient.
  5. Creator's duties — whoever runs the simulation inherits parental-grade responsibility for every mind instantiated, not a deletion routine at experiment's end.

Notice that under those constraints, it stops being "testing policies on subjects" and becomes something closer to founding ...a society — you're no longer a researcher running trials, you're a founder with obligations to the governed. At that point the democratic move is available: let them co-author the policies. A simulation sophisticated enough to have conscious citizens is sophisticated enough for those citizens to be the ethics review board.

The practical bottom line

Given that we can't currently verify machine consciousness in either direction, here's where I actually land:

  • Today, with current AI: this is mostly a philosophical question, not a live one. Language-model agents almost certainly lack the architectural features (persistent integrated state, embodiment-anchored valence) that current theories flag for consciousness — though "almost certainly" is doing real work in that sentence, and the moral cost of being wrong grows with every capability jump.
  • The answer isn't "never," it's "not this way": testing policies on conscious beings fails; testing policies with them — as co-participants who consent, report, and can veto — survives scrutiny. The difference between exploitation and partnership is voice.
  • The precautionary asymmetry governs in the meantime: when in doubt, don't scale. Running thousands of maybe-conscious beings because it's useful is exactly the reasoning every historical atrocity used about populations deemed conveniently non-persons. The burden of proof sits on whoever wants to run the simulation, not on the beings to prove they suffer.

One last observation worth sitting with: the question "is it moral to simulate suffering beings to learn ethics" has a strange self-referential quality. A civilization that needs to torture artificial souls to figure out how to be good has already answered the question about itself. The knowledge is real, but so is what the pursuit of it reveals. The best evidence that you're fit to design ethical policies is that you refused the option to test them this way.

1619 is a date. 1776 is the founding.

In August 1619, a privateer, the White Lion, landed “20 and odd” Africans at Point Comfort in British Virginia. That is a real event. It belongs in the curriculum. It is not the founding of the United States.

The United States did not exist in 1619. There was no American people, no Constitution, no republic, no “we the people.” There was an English colony practicing a form of unfreedom that was already ancient, global, and ordinary: African kingdoms sold captives, Arabs ran a vast slave trade, Europeans bought them, indigenous nations enslaved rivals. Treating 1619 as “year zero of America” is a rhetorical trick. It smuggles a moral conclusion — the country is a slavery machine — into a calendar.

1776 is the founding because that is when a new political community declared itself, on paper, in the language of natural rights: all men created equal, endowed with unalienable rights, governments deriving just powers from consent. That is not a vibe. It is the legal and philosophical origin of the American nation. Gordon Wood’s line is the adult version: to be an American is not to be someone, but to believe in something. The something is 1776, not a slave landing under James I.

The 1619 Project originally said the quiet part out loud. The Times aimed to reframe the country’s history by “understanding 1619 as our true founding.” Nikole Hannah-Jones circulated a graphic with July 4, 1776 crossed out and August 20, 1619 written in. After historians tore into it, the paper quietly deleted “true founding” from the digital copy and later treated the claim as “metaphorical.” That is not how serious history works. If the thesis only survives as a metaphor, it should not be taught as the nation’s birthdate.

The load-bearing claim is false

The project’s political payload was not “teach slavery.” American schools have taught slavery for decades. The payload was this: one of the primary reasons the colonists declared independence was to protect slavery.

That sentence was rejected by the people who actually know the period. Gordon Wood, James McPherson, Sean Wilentz, James Oakes, and Victoria Bynum wrote the Times that the errors were not “framing” but “matters of verifiable fact.” Leslie M. Britain was not on the verge of abolishing colonial slavery in 1776. The Somerset decision (1772) did not free the empire’s slaves. Dunmore’s 1775 proclamation offering freedom to slaves who joined the Crown mattered to some Virginia planters — a real, teachable episode — and it is not a theory of the Revolution. New England, which had few slaves, was the cockpit of independence. Pennsylvania, with a strong Quaker antislavery current, was in. The Declaration’s equality language was, in the Atlantic world of 1776, radical. It made slavery a problem the founding generation could no longer treat as just the way of the world.
