**usage_tokens:** 3667

**A:**

You usually can’t determine that with certainty from the text alone. You need an interpretive framework. Common approaches are:

1. **Context and genre**  
   Read each command in its historical, literary, and social context. Some “commands” are situational, legal, rhetorical, or symbolic rather than universal.

2. **Hierarchy of principles**  
   Treat the text as containing both specific rules and deeper moral aims. When commands conflict, prioritize the broader principles the tradition says are central, such as justice, mercy, compassion, or human dignity.

3. **Canonical interpretation**  
   Interpret ambiguous or contradictory passages through the tradition’s accepted core teachings, later commentary, or more authoritative sections of the text.

4. **Internal consistency test**  
   Prefer the interpretation that makes the overall moral system least self-contradictory. If one command conflicts with many others, it may be local, conditional, or misread.

5. **Tradition and community**  
   Ask how the relevant faith community has historically resolved the tension. Sacred texts are rarely interpreted in isolation.

6. **Moral discernment outside the text**  
   Some believers hold that reason, conscience, and observed moral consequences are part of how divine commands are recognized. If a reading produces cruelty or incoherence, they may treat that reading as mistaken.

A practical rule is: **don’t assume every apparent command is equally binding or equally universal**. First ask whether it is contextual, metaphorical, overridden by a higher principle, or simply a human editorial layer.
