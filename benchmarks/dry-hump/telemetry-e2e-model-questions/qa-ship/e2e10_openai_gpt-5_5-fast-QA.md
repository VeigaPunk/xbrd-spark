A good test is: would a careful editor, public-interest lawyer, and ethics review panel agree that the deception was necessary, proportionate, and likely to prevent more harm than it causes?

So the short answer is: yes, deception can be ethical in undercover journalism, but only as a last resort, with narrow scope, strong evidence of public interest, and serious protections against unnecessary harm.

## Q64

**Q:** Should a person forgive a remorseful offender if refusing forgiveness helps preserve necessary moral boundaries?

**A:**

Yes, but not as a moral obligation.

Forgiveness and boundary-setting solve different problems. If an offender is truly remorseful, forgiveness can be appropriate because it releases resentment and recognizes their moral change. But if refusing forgiveness is what preserves necessary moral boundaries, then withholding forgiveness can also be justified.

The key distinction is this:

- You can acknowledge remorse without granting reconciliation.
- You can drop revenge without offering absolution.
- You can preserve your own moral standards without being vindictive.

So the best answer is: forgive if you can do so without weakening the boundaries that protect you or others. If forgiveness would blur those boundaries, delay it or refuse it. Morally, the priority is not to force forgiveness, but to act in a way that is just, honest, and protective of what matters.
